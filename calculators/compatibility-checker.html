How the checks work
How is loudness worked out?
We take the amplifier's power into the speaker's nominal impedance, convert it to the voltage the speaker sees, and add that to the speaker's sensitivity (measured at 2.83 V from 1 m). We then subtract the loss over your listening distance (6 dB per doubling) and add 3 dB for a stereo pair. A result of 100 dB or more at the listening position means loud, dynamic music has headroom. Between 92 and 100 dB is fine for moderate listening, and under 92 dB is likely to run out of steam. Room reflections add a little extra, so these figures are slightly conservative.
Why does the power check use the speaker maker's recommended range?
Speaker makers publish a range of amplifier power they have tested their speakers with. We flag an amplifier that falls well under it as a poor match, one just under as a caution, and one far above as a caution because a very powerful amplifier can damage the speakers if driven hard. Anything inside the range, or moderately above it, is fine.
What does the impedance check look for?
Speakers rated at 4 Ω or lower, or that dip below 3 Ω at some frequencies, ask an amplifier for a lot of current. If an amplifier's output rises well between 8 Ω and 4 Ω (by about 40% or more), that is a good sign it can supply that current. If the 4 Ω figure barely rises, or isn't published, we flag it so you can check with the manufacturer. Our speaker impedance guide explains why.
How does the cartridge check decide the gain?
It works out the gain needed to lift the cartridge's output to a 200 mV line level, the same assumption as our phono stage gain calculator, and compares it with the nearest gain setting on the phono stage. Within a few dB either way is a good match. Much lower means a quiet, noisy result; much higher risks overload. For loading, moving-magnet cartridges expect about 47 kΩ, and moving-coil cartridges want a load at or above the maker's recommendation.
