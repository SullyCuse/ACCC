Headphones: Impedance, Sensitivity, and Amplifier Matching

A headphone’s impedance and sensitivity decide what it needs from a source, and whether a separate headphone amp will make any difference at all. High-impedance headphones need voltage; planars need current. Mismatching leads to distortion, hiss, or simply quiet playback.

Headphone specifications look similar to speaker specifications — impedance, sensitivity, frequency response — but they are used and interpreted differently. A speaker amplifier rated at 100 watts is usually far too powerful for headphones; a phone's headphone output is often too weak for high-impedance audiophile headphones. And "you need an amp for those" is one of the most repeated pieces of advice in headphone circles: right often enough that people stop questioning it, wrong often enough that many listeners buy a box that makes no audible difference. Two numbers printed on almost every spec sheet decide which side of the line your headphones fall on.

Impedance: High vs Low

Key Spec

Headphone Impedance (Ω)

Consumer and portable headphones and in-ear monitors typically measure 16–64 Ω. Studio and audiophile headphones commonly range from 80 to 600 Ω. The impedance affects how much voltage is needed to drive the headphone to a given sound pressure level. A 300 Ω headphone needs six times the voltage of a 50 Ω headphone to produce the same current through the voice coil. High-impedance headphones therefore need an amplifier capable of higher output voltage — a specification rarely emphasised in consumer electronics, but one that determines whether the amplifier can drive the headphone to satisfying volume levels.

Low-impedance headphones (16–32 Ω) are generally easier to drive to loud levels from a phone or laptop headphone output, but they draw more current. They are also more sensitive to the output impedance of the source, which is covered below. High-impedance designs (150–600 Ω) typically offer lower distortion at high signal levels and are more tolerant of output impedance mismatches, but need a source that can swing enough voltage.

Sensitivity: Two Different Rating Systems

Key Spec

Sensitivity: dB/mW vs dB/V

Headphone sensitivity is specified in one of two ways: dB SPL per milliwatt (dB/mW) or dB SPL per volt (dB/V). These are not interchangeable. For a 32 Ω headphone, 100 dB/mW equals 115 dB/V. For a 300 Ω headphone, 100 dB/mW equals only 107 dB/V. Always check which convention the manufacturer uses before comparing sensitivity figures across products with different impedances. The dB/V convention is more useful for matching to a source amplifier, since amplifiers are voltage sources. The dB/mW convention tells you about efficiency relative to power.

A useful rule of thumb: to achieve 110 dB SPL (a reasonable maximum listening level), subtract the sensitivity figure in dB/V from 110, divide the result by 20, and raise 10 to that power. The answer is how many volts RMS the amplifier must deliver. A headphone rated 105 dB/V needs 105/20 ≈ 1.8 V RMS for 110 dB SPL. A headphone rated 95 dB/V needs 1015/20 ≈ 5.6 V RMS — well beyond any phone and more than many compact desktop sources can supply. High-impedance, low-sensitivity headphones (Sennheiser HD 800: 102 dB/V at 300 Ω) require a dedicated desktop amplifier with meaningful output voltage capability.

Voltage Versus Current

Read together, impedance and sensitivity sort headphones into a few practical groups, and explain why two headphones that both "need an amp" can need completely different things.

  • High sensitivity, low impedance (most IEMs and portable on-ears): the easy case. Any modern source drives them to painful volumes with headroom left over.
  • High impedance, moderate sensitivity (classic 250–600 Ω studio headphones): a voltage problem. They draw little current, but reaching 110 dB peaks needs a voltage swing that small outputs cannot produce, and a phone jack often cannot reach comfortable levels at all.
  • Low impedance, low sensitivity (many planar magnetics): a current problem. The voltage needed is unremarkable, but the amp has to push a lot of current through that low impedance to keep the driver under control on bass-heavy, dynamic material.

The third group fools people, because "it plays loud enough" is not the same as "it is being driven properly". A weak output may reach the volume on paper but go soft and lifeless on dynamic peaks.

Driver Technology

Planar Magnetics: Low Impedance, Low Sensitivity

Planar magnetic headphones use a thin membrane with embedded conductors suspended between magnets, rather than a voice coil in a magnetic gap. They typically have low impedance (20–50 Ω) and low sensitivity (85–95 dB/V), so they need an amplifier that can deliver both significant current (for the low impedance) and significant voltage swing (for the low sensitivity). Many planar magnetics are described as "hard to drive" not because of their impedance but because of the combination of low sensitivity and the current required. A desktop amplifier with at least 1 W of output into their impedance is usually a minimum requirement. The planar vs dynamic guide covers the driver types in more depth.

Do You Need a Headphone Amp?

Strip away the marketing and an amplifier does one job: it takes a small audio signal and produces a larger copy with enough voltage to reach the volume you want, and enough current at that voltage, without the signal sagging or distorting. Every device with a headphone output already contains one — your phone, your laptop, your audio interface. So the question is never "amp or no amp". It is whether the amp built into your source can supply what your particular headphones demand at the levels you listen. A dedicated headphone amp is only an upgrade when the built-in one falls short.

You can work it out on paper: use the sensitivity calculation above to find the voltage your headphones need for 105–110 dB peaks, then check what the amp delivers into your headphones' impedance. If it comfortably exceeds the need, you are covered. You can also hear it. The signs that a source is running out of steam are fairly consistent:

  • It never gets loud enough. The volume is near maximum and the music is still merely adequate. This is the classic high-impedance-into-a-phone symptom: the amp has run out of voltage.
  • The bass is thin, soft, or vague. Low frequencies demand the most current. When an amp is current-starved, bass is the first thing to lose weight and grip.
  • Dynamics fall flat. Quiet-to-loud swings feel compressed, and busy passages turn congested or harsh. The amp is clipping or sagging on peaks it cannot cleanly reproduce.
  • Pushing for more level brings strain, not music. The output stage is at its limit.

The caveat that saves money: if your headphones already play as loud as you would ever want, with tight bass and full dynamic swings that do not collapse on peaks, they are being driven properly. An amp cannot improve on "already loud and clean". Easy IEMs and sensitive, low-impedance headphones almost always land here straight out of a phone or laptop.

DAC or amp? A DAC turns the digital file into an analog waveform; an amp drives that waveform into your headphones. A "DAC/amp" is simply one box that does both. When a source sounds weak or lifeless, the amplifier stage is usually the limit, not the DAC: modern conversion is very good even in inexpensive chips, so it is rarely the DAC in a mid-range phone that holds back a hard-to-drive headphone. If your headphones are underpowered, what you need is more clean output, whether it arrives as a standalone amp or as the amp half of a DAC/amp.

Diminishing returns. An amp that supplies clean voltage and current with low output impedance is doing everything an amp can do. Once your headphones are properly driven, a more expensive amp does not extract more detail or widen the stage; those qualities live in the headphones and the recording. The jump from underpowered to adequately powered is large and obvious; the jump from adequate to lavish, for a properly matched pair, is not.

Output Impedance: The Critical Amplifier Spec

Critical Compatibility Spec

Amplifier Output Impedance and the 1/8 Rule

The amplifier's output impedance forms a voltage divider with the headphone's impedance — which is not a flat number but varies with frequency, especially in multi-driver IEMs and many dynamic headphones. When the output impedance is a meaningful fraction of the headphone's, those impedance swings turn into audible changes in frequency response, often a loose or altered bass region. The standard guideline, borrowed from loudspeaker amplifier design, is that the amplifier's output impedance should be no more than 1/8th of the headphone's minimum impedance. For a 300 Ω headphone, the amplifier output impedance should be ≤37 Ω. For a 32 Ω headphone, it must be ≤4 Ω, and a sensitive multi-driver IEM wants well under 1 Ω.

Many consumer electronics have high output impedances from their headphone jacks: some portable media players measure 10–25 Ω. The symptom is not a lack of power but a subtly wrong tonal balance that changes from headphone to headphone, which is why the same high-output-impedance amp can make one pair sound bloated and another thin. A dedicated headphone amplifier typically has an output impedance well below 1 Ω.

Hiss from background noise: A sensitive low-impedance headphone (16–32 Ω, 110+ dB/mW) will reveal the noise floor of almost any amplifier. If you hear hiss at zero volume, the amplifier's noise is being exposed by the headphone's high sensitivity. More gain than needed for comfortable listening is the usual cause.

Frequency Response and Target Curves

A headphone's frequency response is not meant to be flat in the way a loudspeaker's is. Sound from speakers reaches your eardrum after being shaped by your head, shoulders and outer ear; a headphone bypasses much of that path, so its response has to recreate the effect. Researchers have proposed several "target curves" for doing so. The diffuse-field target models sound arriving from all directions, while the Harman target, developed through listener-preference research, adds a modest bass shelf and adjusts the upper midrange; it has become a common reference in measurements.

Two cautions apply when reading headphone graphs. First, measurements are made on artificial ears and head simulators, and results differ between rigs, so compare graphs from the same source. Second, your own ear shape changes what reaches your eardrum, especially above a few kilohertz. A measured treble peak may or may not be audible to you in the same way. Graphs are best used to spot large departures, such as a big bass boost or a strong peak around 3–8 kHz that often reads as harshness or sibilance.

Fit, Seal and Comfort

How a headphone sits on your head changes how it sounds. Closed-back and many over-ear designs rely on a good seal around the ear for their bass: glasses arms, hair, or a loose clamp can break the seal and drain bass noticeably. Ear pads matter acoustically as well as for comfort, since their depth, material and shape affect the distance and angle of the driver to your ear. Replacement pads from a different manufacturer can therefore change the sound as well as the feel.

Comfort decides whether a headphone gets used. Weight, clamping force, headband padding and pad material (velour breathes, leather and protein leather seal better but run warmer) all matter over a long listening session. If possible, wear a pair for at least half an hour before buying. The open-back vs closed-back guide covers how cup design shapes both sound and isolation.

Balanced Headphone Connections

Many headphone amplifiers and portable players now offer a balanced output, usually as a 4.4 mm Pentaconn jack, a 4-pin XLR, or the older 2.5 mm TRRS. In a balanced headphone connection, each earcup's driver is driven by its own pair of amplifier outputs, with no ground wire shared between left and right.

The practical benefits are specific. A balanced output typically delivers twice the voltage of the same device's single-ended output, which helps with high-impedance or low-sensitivity headphones. Removing the shared ground also eliminates a source of crosstalk between channels. It is not a different sound in itself; a single-ended output that already has enough voltage and low output impedance will not be improved simply by switching connectors. Using a balanced output does require a headphone cable wired for it, and many headphones with detachable cables can be fitted with one.

Wireless and USB Headphones

Bluetooth and USB headphones change the compatibility picture completely. They contain their own DAC and amplifier, designed for those specific drivers, so impedance, sensitivity and source output impedance no longer need matching. Sound quality then depends on the headphone's internal electronics and, for Bluetooth, the codec in use. SBC is universal; AAC works well with Apple devices; aptX variants and LDAC offer higher bit rates where both ends support them. Active noise cancellation adds processing that most listeners accept for travel and commuting, at some cost to absolute fidelity.

Listening Safely

Headphones make it easy to listen loudly for long periods without noticing, because there is no room or neighbour to signal how loud it is. The occupational guideline from NIOSH (the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) is 85 dBA for eight hours, with the safe time halving for every 3 dB increase: 88 dBA for four hours, 94 dBA for one hour, and so on. Ringing in the ears or a muffled feeling after listening are signs of temporary over-exposure. Choosing headphones with good isolation helps here, because you are less tempted to raise the volume to cover background noise.
